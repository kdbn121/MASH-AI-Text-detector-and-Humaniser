The tide clock on the harbour wall was made by a clockmaker who worked in brass and
had no training in astronomy. He knew the moon well enough, though, and every month
he set the dial by hand from tables he copied out of an almanac. The mechanism behind
the dial was a simple float in a sealed cylinder. Seawater filled the cylinder as the
tide rose, and the float rose with it, turning a small wheel.

Visitors often assume the clock is electronic. It is not. Each spring the keeper
takes the mechanism apart, dries the cylinder and clears the salt that has collected
in the screw threads. The job takes an afternoon. The clock loses time in the same
way every winter, and the keeper resets it every spring without fuss.

What interests me about the thing is not the clock itself. It is that a device built
for one purpose outlasted the trade that made it. Nobody orders brass tide clocks
now, and nobody learns the job. The knowledge survives because one person at a time
still climbs the tower, and still knows which screw to undo first.
